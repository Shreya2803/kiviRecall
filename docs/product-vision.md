# Product Vision — Semantic Memory for Kivi

## What I noticed

Every product that remembers has decided, usually silently, what relationship it wants
with its user. The relationship is what licenses the memory.

A keyboard wants to be muscle memory, so it learns your vocabulary and nothing else. A
note-taker wants to be a scribe, so it keeps a faithful record and does not interpret
you. A general assistant wants to be a confidant, and that is what permits it to hold
your moods, your health, your relationships — most of it stored without being asked.

Kivi is currently a keyboard. Styles and phonetic memory are muscle memory: they make
dictation yours without knowing anything about you. Semantic memory is the decision
about which relationship Kivi moves into next.

It should become a scribe, not a confidant.

## What Kivi should become

The durable record of a person's work, which they can ask questions of.

## Where the value is created

Kivi hears everything a person authors, in every application. Slack remembers Slack.
Gmail remembers Gmail. Nothing else in the operating system sees the whole of what
someone produces in a day.

So the value is not personalisation. It is continuity across applications. A person
decides something in Slack on Monday, explains it in an email on Wednesday, and by the
time it matters in March it survives only as three fragments they cannot find. Kivi is
the only thing positioned to hold all three and return them as one answer.

That is worth more than a system that knows the user's hobbies.

## What deserves to be remembered

What a person authored that outlives the moment of authoring: projects and their state,
who owns what, decisions and when they changed, commitments and deadlines, and the
vocabulary particular to this person's work — project names, colleagues, vendors,
acronyms.

These share two properties. The person will need them again, and they are scattered.

## What Kivi must never assume

That hearing someone speak is permission to model them.

Kivi must never infer from *how* a person speaks — mood, stress, health, confidence,
personality. Dictation captures people thinking aloud; they say things to their laptop
they would never type into a search box. A speech company is unusually well placed to
make these inferences, which is exactly why declining is a position rather than a
limitation.

Kivi must never build memory about third parties. Dictation is full of candid remarks
about colleagues who are not users and did not consent. Kivi records that Priya owns the
design system. It does not record what you think of her.

Kivi must never treat one ambiguous mention as settled fact, and must never silently
change what dictation writes. Regular dictation has one contract: what you said is what
appears. Memory may feed learned names into phonetic correction, or offer a visible
suggestion. It never rewrites.

## Why someone should trust this enough to keep using it

Three things, all inspectable.

Everything Kivi knows sits in one place, in plain language, beside the dictations that
produced it. Every answer cites the words the person actually said. And when the history
does not contain an answer, Kivi says so and names what it does have, instead of
assembling something plausible.

Control lives where the memory appears — correct it, pin it, or forget it at the point
you see it, not in a settings page. Forgetting survives reprocessing.

## What this position forbids

Emotional-state inference. A social graph of colleagues. Proactive memory that surfaces
uninvited. A personality model that reshapes output. Cross-user learning.

Each is buildable. Each would turn Kivi into something people install once and then
watch.
